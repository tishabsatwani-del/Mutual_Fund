The five questions to ask any return

  1. Whose maths is it? The fund’s, or mine?
  2. Which window am I being shown? And what do the other windows say?
  3. After the bill, what is left? Inflation is the return working against you.
  4. What did it cost to hold? The fall along the way, not just the finish.
  5. Who kept it? The fund’s return and the investor’s are not the same number.

Every tool below answers one or more of these. Nothing here rates a fund, ranks one, or tells you to buy, sell, switch or hold anything.

Words this tool keeps apart
XIRR
What your money earned, counting the date every rupee went in and came out.
CAGR
The annual growth between two values. It ignores everything that happened in between, and every payment you made along the way.
Rolling return
The annualised return of every historical holding period of a given length — not one lucky stretch.
Absolute return
Total gain or loss, not spread over years. 50% over ten years and 50% over one year are very different things.
NAV
Net asset value — a fund's price per unit on a given day. A file of those, day by day, is its history.
Index
A measure of a whole market. A total return index (TRI) counts dividends; a price index does not, and reads lower.
Benchmark
The index a fund is measured against. A reference point, not a verdict.

Where do I get an index file?

Download the historical values of the index from the index provider’s own site — for Indian equity indices that is NSE Indices. Ask for the longest period they offer.

The file needs two columns and nothing else matters: a date, and the index value on that date. Any of 05-08-2026, 05-Aug-2026 or 2026-08-05 will be read. Extra columns are ignored.

Prefer a Total Return Index (TRI) file where one is offered. A TRI counts dividends; a price index does not, and will read several percent a year lower than what an investor actually received.

Or load the fund’s NAV file below.

NAV is the fund’s price per unit on a given day. A file of those, day by day, is all this needs.

CSV or Excel · a date column and a NAV column is all it needs

Official downloads call these “schemes”, and hold every one of them in a single file. Check the plan and option — Direct and Regular of the same fund are different things, and so are Growth and IDCW.

Where do I get my fund’s NAV file?

Download the NAV history from AMFI or your fund house for the period you want. Load the file exactly as it arrives — if it holds hundreds of funds, you pick yours above.

It reads AMFI’s semicolon files, ordinary comma files and Excel workbooks. Dates can be 05-08-2026, 05-Aug-2026 or 2026-08-05. Rows it cannot read are counted and shown to you rather than quietly dropped.

What is inside

  • My investments — every payment you made, the XIRR your own money earned, what you put in, what it is worth now, and how long you have held it. Name up to five holdings and each is measured on its own, beside the portfolio total.
  • Plan my goal — what you are aiming for, where you land if nothing changes, and the extra each month that would close the gap. Four scenarios beside it.
  • Example — the same thing already filled in, if you get stuck.
  • About — the version, and what the figures do and do not include.

The goal tab gives the same answers as on this site — the two were checked against each other.

How to use it

  1. Download the file and open it in Excel. Save your own copy first, so you always have a clean one.
  2. On the My investments tab, enter one row for every payment you have made. Date, then choose Money in from the dropdown, then the amount. Always type the amount as a plain positive number.
  3. If you have taken money out, enter that as a row too, choosing Money out.
  4. In the last row, enter today's date, choose Worth today, and type what the holding is worth right now.

Your XIRR appears at the top of the sheet. That is the yearly rate your own money has earned.

If something looks wrong

  • No answer yet. Read the line under the result. It will tell you what is still missing, usually the Worth today row.
  • The date looks odd. The sheet shows dates as 05-Aug-2026. If yours reads 08-May-2026, your computer is set to American dates. Retype the date the other way round.
  • A number will not go in. Type digits only, with no rupee sign and no commas.
  • No Excel? Upload the file to Google Sheets and it works there too, unchanged.

Sheet or screen?

Both run the same calculation and give the same answer. The sheet is a record you keep and add to. is quicker for a one-off look, handles withdrawals across several funds at once, and explains what the number means as it goes.

The sheet's version number and build date are on its About tab — spreadsheet version 1.2, separate from the tool’s own version.

How the numbers are worked out

So you can check them, argue with them, or reproduce them yourself.

XIRR

Every payment you make counts as money out, and every rupee you take back — including what the holding is worth today — counts as money in. The tool finds the single annual rate at which all of those, discounted from their own dates, add up to zero. A 365-day year is used, matching the convention a spreadsheet uses, so the answer here and the answer in Excel agree.

The search is a bisection, not a Newton step. Newton is faster but wanders off on deeply negative cases, which is exactly when someone anxious is looking at the screen.

Median, not mean

Where this tool prints a middle figure it is the median — the window with half above it and half below. A chapter that says “average” means the mean, which is a different number.

The median is used because a handful of exceptional stretches cannot pull it upward. A mean can be lifted by a few spectacular windows into a figure no ordinary holding period ever produced.

Rolling returns

A window starts at every single observation in the file. It ends on the same calendar date a chosen number of years later. If the market was shut that day, the most recent earlier price is used, but only within seven days — beyond that the window is dropped rather than stretched, because stretching quietly measures a different holding period than the one you asked for.

Each window is annualised as the compound rate that links its two ends. An average of yearly returns is a different statistic and is never reported here in its place.

Goal projections

The monthly rate is the twelfth root of the annual rate, not the annual rate divided by twelve. Dividing by twelve overstates the outcome, and across twenty years the overstatement is not small. Instalments are treated as paid at the start of each month, which is how a mandate actually runs, and a step-up is applied once every twelve months.

Every projection uses a return you typed in. It is an assumption, not a forecast, and nothing here should be read as a promise about the future.

Checking the engine

The calculations are covered by a test suite that compares them against values worked out independently — closed-form formulas, cases done by hand, and results verified in a spreadsheet outside this tool. A test that only agrees with the code it is testing proves nothing.

About and privacy

This page runs the book’s five questions on your own money. You enter what you paid in and took out; it fetches the fund’s public NAV history, for any fund, including ones launched after this book was printed, and works everything out on your phone. Nothing you type leaves it. It does not rate funds, rank them, or tell you what to buy, sell or switch. It tells you where you stand and what to check next. Every sentence it shows is tied to a number it has just calculated, so the same figures will get the same sentence in ten years. It compares your fund with an index fund you could actually have bought, not with the index itself. Figures are before tax and exit load. It describes what has already happened and predicts nothing.

The fifth question: who kept it?

Between 2003 and 2022, a stretch containing two of the deepest crashes this market has produced, Indian equity funds delivered about 19.1% a year. The people invested in those same funds earned about 13.8%.

Same funds, same twenty years. The gap went to no fee, no tax and no line on any statement. Part of it is innocent arithmetic — in a fast-growing fund industry most rupees arrive late by definition. The rest lived inside two dates: the day people arrived, and the day they left.

This is the one number on this page that is not about a fund. Every other figure here is decided in boardrooms and budgets you do not control. This one is decided on a phone, late at night, with a thumb over one button.

What this is not

This is an educational tool for readers of the book. It reports on money that has already moved and illustrates assumptions you choose yourself. It does not recommend funds, rate them, or tell you what to buy, sell or switch. Figures are before exit load and before tax. It is not investment advice.
